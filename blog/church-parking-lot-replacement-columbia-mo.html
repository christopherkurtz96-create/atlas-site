Church Parking Lot Replacement in Columbia, MO: Planning and Budget for Churches and Schools

What it costs, how to budget and pay for it, and how the work gets scheduled around Sunday services and summer break. Written for trustees, facilities chairs and school business managers around Columbia.

November 26, 2026 10 min read

"What's it going to cost, and can we still have church on Sunday?" Those are the two questions I hear from every trustee, facilities chair and school business manager once the lot finally has to come out. Short answer: a church parking lot replacement around Columbia runs about $4 to $10 per square foot in asphalt, removed and repaved, and yes, you keep meeting. I'm Chris Kurtz, owner of ATLAS Demolition & Site Work in Columbia, MO. Here's how to plan it, budget it and get it through a board vote without surprises.

Quick Answer: A church parking lot replacement in Columbia, MO costs about $4 to $10 per square foot for asphalt, removed, rebuilt and repaved. That puts a 15,000 square foot lot with about 40 spaces at $60,000 to $150,000, and nearly all of that swing is the condition of the base. Concrete costs roughly twice as much for the surface and lasts 30 years or more. Churches get worked Monday through Friday and reopened for Sunday. Schools get done over summer break. Start planning 6 to 12 months ahead, get a test hole dug before you set the budget, and ask for removal, base and surface as separate lines.

What a Church Parking Lot Replacement Costs in Columbia, MO

A full replacement is not a sealcoat or an overlay. The old surface comes out, the rock base gets fixed or rebuilt, the grade gets corrected so water leaves, and a new surface goes down with curbs and striping. On our parking lot removal and replacement jobs, asphalt lots removed and repaved land between $4 and $10 per square foot around Mid-Missouri.

Here's what a church parking lot replacement looks like at that range, on the lot sizes most churches and schools have. A parking space with its share of the drive lane takes up roughly 375 square feet, so you can get close by counting stalls.

Lot Size About How Many Spaces Asphalt, Removed and Repaved
10,000 sq ft 25 to 30 $40,000 to $100,000
15,000 sq ft 40 $60,000 to $150,000
30,000 sq ft 80 $120,000 to $300,000
60,000 sq ft 160 $240,000 to $600,000

A few lines sit on top of the per-square-foot number, and each one gets its own place on the proposal:

  • Curb and gutter: $25 to $45 per linear foot.
  • Striping and ADA signage: $800 to $3,500.
  • Disposal and recycling: $40 to $90 per ton, billed at actual tonnage with the tickets attached.

Concrete costs roughly twice as much as asphalt to install, and then it mostly gets left alone for 30 years. Most church and school lots we price end up as a mix: asphalt where the cars park, concrete where the heavy vehicles turn. That usually lands 10 to 20 percent above an all-asphalt price. Our post on asphalt vs concrete parking lots walks through that choice.

Why the Base Sets the Budget

The widest swing in any church parking lot replacement is the part nobody can see from the office. A lot with a sound rock base that only needs a new surface costs about half of one whose base has turned to mud and has to be dug out and rebuilt. That's the whole difference between the low end and the high end of the table above.

So before anyone votes on a number, find out what's under there. On the site visit I core or dig a test hole in the worst area and one in a decent area. It takes a few minutes, and it turns a guess into a proposal built on what's actually in the ground.

If your board is still arguing about whether the lot can be patched for a few more years, read our post on parking lot replacement vs repair first. Alligator cracking, patches that keep failing and standing water are base problems, and a new surface over a bad base is money spent twice.

How to Build the Budget for a Board Vote

Trustees and school boards don't like surprises, and a church parking lot replacement has more ways to surprise you than most projects. Here's the order I'd work in.

  1. Get the test hole first. Don't set a budget from a price per square foot off the internet. Set it from what the base looks like.
  2. Ask for separate lines. Removal, base, surface, curbs and striping should each have their own number. If a bid is one lump sum, you can't tell what happens to the price when the base turns out worse than expected.
  3. Carry a contingency. I'd tell any board to hold 10 to 15 percent for base repairs that only show up once the surface is off. If you don't spend it, it goes back in the reserve.
  4. Add a disposal allowance. Hauling is billed at actual tonnage, so the proposal shows an estimate. Budget to the high side of it.
  5. Pick the surface on purpose. Have it priced in asphalt, in concrete, and as a mix. The cheapest option today is not always the cheapest over 30 years.
  6. List the extras now. New storm inlets, light pole bases, a wider drop-off lane or extra accessible stalls are all cheaper during a replacement than after it. Decide before the vote, not during the job.

The reserve math: an asphalt surface lasts 15 to 20 years with upkeep. If your lot would cost $100,000 to replace, that's $5,000 to $7,000 a year you should be setting aside. A church that starts that line item the year the new lot opens never has to run an emergency campaign for the next one.

How Churches and Schools Pay for It

I'm a contractor, not a finance committee, but I see the same four approaches on almost every church parking lot replacement:

  • Building or capital reserve. The cleanest way. The money is there and the vote is about timing.
  • A short capital campaign. A parking lot is an easy thing to give toward. Everybody in the congregation has hit the same pothole.
  • A loan. Common when the lot can't wait and the reserve is thin.
  • Two budget years. The worst section gets rebuilt this year and the rest next year. Each stage carries its own price on the proposal. It costs a little more in total because the crews mobilize twice, but it keeps a six-figure project inside two annual budgets.

Private and parochial schools usually work the same way a church does. Public school districts are different. In Missouri, a district generally has to advertise larger construction projects for sealed bids, and prevailing wage rules apply to public work above a state threshold. Your business office knows the current numbers. What it means for the calendar is that a public school lot needs its scope written, bid and approved months before the summer it gets built. We bid owner-direct, and we'll bid to a general contractor when the lot is part of a bigger project.

Scheduling a Church Lot Around Sunday

A church lot has a rhythm no other property has. It's nearly empty six days a week and full for three hours on the seventh. That makes a church parking lot replacement one of the easier jobs to schedule, as long as the plan respects Sunday.

  • Monday through Friday. We work the week and clear out for the weekend. Equipment is parked out of the way, the open section is swept and striped, and the closed section is barricaded.
  • Two or three sections. Only one is closed at a time. The entrance and the rows nearest the door stay open the longest.
  • Accessible stalls never go away. If the permanent ones are in the section being torn out, temporary ones are set up close to the door before the old ones close.
  • Funerals and midweek events. Tell us as soon as you know. We'll pull back and open what we can.
  • The church calendar. Most congregations stay away from the weeks around Easter and Christmas. Early summer and early fall are the favorites.

Plan on 2 to 3 weeks for a 15,000 square foot church parking lot replacement in asphalt and 3 to 4 weeks in concrete, with part of the lot open the whole time. The full mechanics are in our post on parking lot replacement phasing.

Scheduling a School Lot Around Summer Break

A school lot is the opposite problem. It can't be half closed while buses and parents are using it, so nearly all of the work has to fit between the last day of school and the first. Around Columbia that's roughly late May to mid-August, and it goes fast.

  • Start the week school lets out. Every day you wait in June comes off the cure time in August.
  • Pour the bus loop first. Buses are heavy and they turn in the same spot twice a day, which ruts asphalt. Bus lanes, drop-off lanes and dumpster pads should be concrete. Concrete needs about 7 days before cars and closer to 28 days before buses and trucks, so the heavy lanes get poured early in the summer.
  • Work around summer use. Summer school, sports camps and the front office all need a way in. That gets drawn on the plan like any other phase.
  • Watch the early-August dates. Fall sports, band camp and teacher work days start before students do. The lot has to be striped and open by then, not by the first day of class.

Because the window is fixed, a school lot should be walked in the fall or winter, priced by early spring, and on the paving schedule before the school year ends.

Fix the Layout While the Lot Is Torn Out

A church parking lot replacement is the one time in 20 or 30 years when the layout can change for almost nothing. Before the plan is final, walk the lot with these in mind:

  • Accessible parking. The count is based on total stalls. Under the federal standards, a lot with 26 to 50 spaces needs 2 accessible spaces, 51 to 75 needs 3, and 76 to 100 needs 4, with at least one of every six being van accessible. The U.S. Access Board's guide to accessible parking has the full table. Churches are treated differently than public schools under the federal ADA, but local building codes generally ask for accessible parking either way, and your older members need those stalls more than anyone. We lay every lot out to the current standard.
  • Drainage. If water stands by the front door or runs across the sidewalk and freezes, the regrade is when that gets fixed.
  • Drop-off and pickup. A covered entrance, a preschool door or a parent pickup line may need a wider lane or a different direction of travel.
  • Lighting and signs. Pole bases and sign bases come out and go back in with the lot. Moving one now is cheap.
  • Fire lane. It gets striped and signed at close-out.

If you're adding stalls, changing an entrance or changing where the water goes, ask the city or county whether they want to review the plan before work starts. A like-for-like replacement is simpler.

Timing a Lot Replacement in Columbia and Mid-Missouri

Two local facts shape the calendar. Asphalt plants in this part of the state run roughly April through November, so paving has to land in that window. And the clay under Columbia, Jefferson City, Fulton and Moberly holds water, which is why so many lots here fail from the base up. Drainage gets corrected as part of our grading and base work, one section at a time, so the new lot sheds water instead of soaking it in.

For a church or a school, a calendar that works looks like this:

  • Fall or winter: site walk, test holes, proposal with the phasing map.
  • Late winter to early spring: board or congregational vote, funding lined up, contract signed.
  • Late spring through early fall: the work, 2 to 4 weeks for most lots.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a church parking lot replacement cost?

Around Columbia and Mid-Missouri, a church parking lot replacement in asphalt runs about $4 to $10 per square foot, removed, rebuilt and repaved. A 15,000 square foot lot with about 40 spaces lands between $60,000 and $150,000. A 30,000 square foot lot with about 80 spaces lands between $120,000 and $300,000. The spread comes from the base. If the rock under the old surface is sound, you're near the low end. If it has turned to mud and has to be dug out and rebuilt, you're near the high end. Concrete costs roughly twice as much for the surface. Curbs, striping and disposal are separate lines on the proposal.

How should a church budget for a new parking lot?

Budget a church parking lot replacement from a test hole, not a guess. Once you know what the base looks like, ask for a written proposal with removal, base, surface, curbs and striping as separate lines. Carry a contingency of 10 to 15 percent for base repairs that only show up once the surface is off, and an allowance for disposal, which is billed at actual tonnage. Then decide how to fund it. Most churches use some mix of a building reserve, a short capital campaign and a loan. If the full number is too much for one year, the lot can be split so the worst section is rebuilt this year and the rest next year.

Can we still hold services during a church parking lot replacement?

Yes. We work Monday through Friday and clear out for the weekend, so the open part of the lot is clean, striped and usable on Sunday morning. The lot is split into two or three sections, and only one is closed at a time. The entrance, the rows closest to the door and the accessible stalls stay available on every phase. If a funeral or a midweek event comes up, tell us and we'll pull equipment back and open what we can. You'll have fewer stalls for two to four weeks, so plan on greeters in the lot and maybe overflow parking at a neighbor's lot for the tightest Sunday.

When is the best time of year to replace a church or school parking lot?

Late spring through early fall. Asphalt plants in this part of Missouri run roughly April through November, and concrete cures best when nights stay above freezing. Schools are almost always done over summer break, starting the week after classes end so concrete bus lanes have time to cure before the first day back. Churches have more room to choose. Most avoid the weeks around Easter and Christmas and aim for early summer or early fall. Removal and base work can be done in winter, and some owners tear out in late winter to be first on the paving schedule in spring. Whatever the season, get on the calendar several months ahead.

How many accessible parking spaces does a church or school lot need?

The count is based on the total number of stalls. Under the federal ADA standards, a lot with 1 to 25 spaces needs 1 accessible space, 26 to 50 needs 2, 51 to 75 needs 3, 76 to 100 needs 4, 101 to 150 needs 5, and 151 to 200 needs 6. At least one of every six accessible spaces has to be van accessible, with a wider access aisle. Those are minimums. A congregation with a lot of older members usually wants more than the minimum near the door, and a church parking lot replacement is the cheapest time to add them. We draw the accessible stalls, aisles, ramps and signs on the plan before anything is poured.
