Patio Removal and Replacement in Columbia, MO: Fixing a Cracked Patio or Sidewalk

When a cracked patio or walk is past saving, how the tear-out goes, what the new pour costs around Columbia, and why the base under it matters more than the concrete on top.

November 20, 2026 9 min read

"Can you just tear out my old patio and pour a new one?" is a question I hear from homeowners around Columbia every week. Short answer: yes, and when the slab has heaved, sunk or cracked all the way through, that's usually the right fix. I'm Chris Kurtz, owner of ATLAS Demolition & Site Work in Columbia, MO. Here's how patio removal and replacement works, what it costs, and when you're better off calling somebody else.

Quick Answer: Patio removal and replacement in Columbia, MO runs $3 to $6 per square foot to break out and haul off the old slab, plus $8 to $12 per square foot for a new 4-inch pour on a compacted rock base. Disposal is passed through at $40 to $90 per ton. The tear-out takes half a day to a day, the pour follows within a few days, and the new concrete needs about 7 days to cure. Cracked sidewalks work the same way, except that a public sidewalk inside city limits needs a right-of-way permit. One contractor can do both halves on one contract.

When a Cracked Patio or Sidewalk Has to Come Out

Not every crack means a tear-out. Concrete cracks. That's what the control joints are for. The slabs that need concrete removal are the ones where the problem is under the concrete, not in it.

Here's what tells me a patio or walk is done:

  • Panels heaved at the joints. One side sits an inch or more above the other. That's usually clay swelling under a slab that never had a real base.
  • Sections sunk and holding water. If it ponds after every rain, the ground under it has moved.
  • The slab drains toward the house. A patio poured against the foundation that now tips back at it is sending every storm to your basement wall.
  • Cracks that go all the way through. Wide, uneven, and growing each winter.
  • Surface scaling across most of the slab. Common on concrete that was poured thin or finished wet, then salted for years.

If what you have is a few hairline cracks or one panel that dropped half an inch, a sealing or leveling company is the right call. We don't do leveling or patch repair, and we'll say so at the site visit. Our rule is simple. When a slab is heaved, cracked through, or sunk more than an inch or two, tear it out, fix the base, and pour it right.

How the Tear-Out Works

On patio removal and replacement, the removal half is the quick half. Most patios and walks are half a day to a day on site.

  1. Site visit. We measure, check the thickness at an edge, and walk the access route. Most patios and walks are 4 inches thick and unreinforced, but we look for mesh and rebar before we price it.
  2. Locates. Missouri 811 gets called before we cut. Private lines are yours to flag. Think irrigation, landscape lighting, and the gas line to the grill.
  3. Saw-cut. Anywhere the old slab meets concrete that's staying, like the back stoop or the driveway, we cut a straight line first. The concrete you keep gets a clean edge and doesn't crack when the rest breaks.
  4. Break and load. Our Cat 308 with a hydraulic breaker does the breaking where it can reach. Any mesh or rebar is cut out and hauled with the concrete.
  5. Haul and recycle. The loads go to a concrete recycler in the Columbia area, where the old patio gets crushed into base rock. It doesn't go to a landfill.

Backyard Access Decides the Price

A driveway sits next to the street. A patio sits behind the house, behind a fence, sometimes down a slope. Getting to the slab is the whole story.

If we can pull two fence panels and drive the excavator in, it's a one-day job at normal rates. If the only way in is a 36-inch gate, we bring a stand-up mini skid steer and carry the concrete out in smaller loads. That adds 20 to 40 percent on the removal side. A pad no machine can reach is a hand-carry job, and that can double it.

Fixing the Base Is the Point

Here's the part most people skip. The old patio didn't fail because the concrete was bad. It failed because of what was under it.

Mid-Missouri sits on clay that swells when it's wet and shrinks when it's dry. A slab poured straight on that clay rides the movement up and down until it cracks. Add a downspout dumping next to it and a few freeze-thaw winters, and you get the heaved, tilted patio you're looking at now.

So once the old concrete is gone, we strip the mud and loose material, regrade so water runs away from the house, and build a compacted rock base. Where the ground is soft, we dig deeper and replace the clay with more rock. Skip that step and patio removal and replacement just buys you the same cracks in a few years.

The Replacement Pour

Most people who call about removing a patio want a patio when we leave. Our concrete crew pours it back under our contract and our warranty, quoted on the same proposal as the tear-out. Here's the standard spec:

  • 4-inch slab on the compacted rock base
  • 4,000 psi air-entrained mix, which is what holds up to freeze-thaw
  • Fiber in the mix or #3 rebar on a 24-inch grid, your call at the proposal
  • Control joints cut every 10 to 12 feet so shrinkage cracks land in the joints
  • An isolation joint or dowels where the new slab meets the house or existing concrete, depending on what's there
  • Broom finish

Then it cures. Give it about 7 days before you load it up. If you'd rather use your own finisher, or you're putting in pavers or a deck instead, that's fine too. We'll leave the subgrade compacted and ready for whoever comes next.

The process is the same one we use on concrete driveway replacement, scaled down. A lot of our patio work gets done on the same trip as a driveway, because the machine and the trucks are already there.

We don't take small stand-alone removals under a couple thousand dollars unless they're next to other work we're doing. One cracked sidewalk panel by itself isn't a fit. A patio, a walk and a stoop together usually is, and so is a short walk bundled with a driveway.

Sidewalks: Yours vs. the City's

There are two kinds of sidewalk, and they're not the same job. That matters when patio removal and replacement includes the walk out front.

A private walk runs from your driveway to the front door or around the side of the house. It's on your lot, and it gets handled exactly like a patio. Saw-cut at the stoop, break it out, fix the base, pour it back.

A public sidewalk runs along the street in the right-of-way. Inside Columbia city limits, replacing it takes a right-of-way permit from the City of Columbia, and the new panels have to be poured to city standards. The panels that cross a driveway are generally 6 inches thick. You can check current requirements on the city's permits and licensing page. When it's part of our job, we pull the permit and pour to spec so the inspection passes the first time.

Outside the city, in unincorporated Boone County and in towns like Ashland, Fulton, Boonville and Jefferson City, the rules for the right-of-way change with the address. The private side doesn't. Patio removal and replacement on your own lot is the same work in every one of them, and we cover all of Mid-Missouri within about 45 minutes of Columbia.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does patio removal and replacement cost in Columbia, MO?

Removal runs $3 to $6 per square foot for a 4-inch patio or walk, and a new 4-inch pour on a compacted rock base runs $8 to $12 per square foot. Disposal is a pass-through at $40 to $90 per ton, billed at actual tonnage with the recycler's tickets attached. A 300-square-foot patio with decent access works out to roughly $3,300 to $5,400 for both halves. What moves the number is access, reinforcement in the old slab, how much base has to be rebuilt, and the size of the job. Small pads carry a mobilization minimum of $600 to $1,000 on the removal side, because the trailer trip and the dump run cost the same either way.

Can a cracked patio be repaired instead of replaced?

Sometimes, and we'll tell you when that's the case. Hairline cracks that aren't moving, light surface wear, or one panel that has settled a little are jobs for a sealing or leveling company. We don't do leveling, mudjacking, crack filling or patch repair. Patio removal and replacement is the right call when the slab is heaved at the joints, cracked all the way through, sunk more than an inch or two, or pitched back toward the house. Those are base problems. Leveling lifts the slab but doesn't fix what's under it, and a lifted slab often cracks or settles again within a few years. At that point you've paid twice.

How long does it take to remove and replace a concrete patio?

The tear-out is fast. Most patios and walks are half a day to a day to saw-cut, break, load and haul. Forms and base go in within a few days of the removal, and the pour happens on the next dry day after that. Then the concrete needs about 7 days of cure before you put heavy loads on it. Figure one to two weeks for patio removal and replacement, from the day we start to the day you're using it, with most of that being cure time and weather. Tight backyard access can add a day on the removal side because everything moves in smaller loads.

Do I need a permit to replace a patio or sidewalk in Columbia, MO?

Tearing out a patio or a private walk on your own lot does not need a demolition permit, inside or outside Columbia city limits. A public sidewalk is different. It sits in the city right-of-way, so replacing it inside Columbia takes a right-of-way permit from the city, and the new panels have to be poured to city standards. We pull that permit and pour to spec when it's part of our job. If you're adding a roof, a screened room or anything structural over the new patio, check with the city's Building and Site Development office before the pour, because that is a separate question from the concrete.

Can you get equipment into a fenced backyard?

Almost always. The question is which machine. If we can pull two fence panels and drive the Cat 308 in, a backyard patio is a one-day removal. If the only way in is a 36-inch gate, we use a stand-up mini skid steer and move the concrete out in smaller loads, which takes longer and adds 20 to 40 percent to the removal side. A pad with no machine access at all is a hand-carry job and can double it. We walk the access route at the site visit, measure the gate, and look at the slope and where the trucks can sit. That's why the firm price comes after the visit and not before.
