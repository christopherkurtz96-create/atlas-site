What the Tear-Out Costs

The removal half of concrete driveway replacement cost gets skipped in most online guides, or it's buried in a national number that assumes a Florida crew with a dump 10 minutes away. Here's what it actually involves around Columbia.

First, the saw cuts. We cut a clean line at the garage apron and where the drive meets the public sidewalk or street, so the concrete that stays keeps a straight, undamaged edge. Then the old slab comes out with the hydraulic breaker on our Cat 308 and goes straight into trucks. Rebar and wire mesh get pulled and scrapped.

The tonnage is where people get surprised. A 700 square foot, 4-inch drive is about 17 tons of concrete. A 6-inch drive with rebar is closer to 26 tons. At $40 to $90 per ton, that's $700 to $2,300 in disposal before anybody's swung a bucket. All of it goes to a crusher in the Columbia area and comes back as base rock instead of going to a landfill, which keeps the per-ton rate at the low end.

Put together, tear-out on a two-car drive usually lands at $2,500 to $5,500 with hauling. If you just need the old slab gone and aren't pouring new, our concrete removal service does that as a stand-alone job, and we break down those numbers in our post on concrete removal cost in Columbia, MO.

What the New Slab Costs

The new pour is the bigger half of concrete driveway replacement cost, and the base under it is where the money either does its job or gets wasted.

The base. This is the part most failed driveways in Columbia never had. We cut the subgrade to depth, compact it, then place 4 inches of crushed limestone and compact that in lifts. Where the old drive settled badly, we go deeper and swap the soft clay for more rock. On a two-car drive the base is roughly $1,500 to $3,000 of the total, and it's the line that decides whether the slab lasts 12 years or 30.

The concrete. Residential standard is a 4-inch slab of 4,000 psi air-entrained mix, reinforced with fiber in the mix or #3 rebar on a 24-inch grid. Rebar adds roughly $1 per square foot over fiber and holds a cracked panel together better. Control joints get cut every 10 to 12 feet so shrinkage cracks land in the joints instead of across a panel. Broom finish for traction. At $8 to $12 per square foot installed, a 700 square foot drive is $5,600 to $8,400 for the slab and base.

Thicker slabs. Drives that carry an RV, a boat trailer, or a loaded work truck get 5 to 6 inches with rebar, at $10 to $14 per square foot. That's about $2 more per foot, or $1,200 to $1,600 on a two-car drive.

Foot traffic after 24 hours, vehicles after 7 days, full design strength at 28. Day 1 is removal and base, day 2 is the pour. Most two-car drives are two working days on site, and that short schedule is part of why concrete driveway replacement cost stays predictable once the bid is written.

What Moves Concrete Driveway Replacement Cost Up or Down

Two driveways with the same square footage can come in $4,000 apart on concrete driveway replacement cost. Here's what makes the difference.

  • Square footage and shape. The obvious one. A 10-foot-wide drive that's 40 feet long is 400 square feet. Widen it to 16 feet for a third stall and you're at 640. Long rural drives are priced by length: a 12-foot-wide drive is 1,200 square feet per 100 feet.
  • How deep the base has to go. If the old drive heaved or settled, the clay under it is the reason. Fixing that means cutting deeper and placing more rock. Add $500 to $2,000 on a bad subgrade.
  • Truck access. If a concrete truck can back right up to the forms, the pour is fast. If it's a tight lot with a fence and trees and the concrete has to be pumped or wheeled, add labor.
  • The apron. The section between the sidewalk and the street is in the public right-of-way. Inside Columbia city limits, that takes a right-of-way permit from the City of Columbia, and the apron and any sidewalk panels across the drive have to be poured to city spec, usually 6 inches thick with the curb cut rebuilt. That's a few hundred dollars in permit and inspection and a thicker pour on that section.
  • Rebar vs. fiber. About $1 per square foot. Worth it on anything that carries real weight.
  • Finish. Broom finish is standard and included. Stamped, colored, or exposed-aggregate finishes are quoted separately and can double the per-foot price of the slab.
  • Gravel to concrete. Converting a gravel drive is often cheaper per foot than replacing concrete, because there's no tear-out and the packed gravel is usually a decent base already. Many owners outside Columbia concrete the first 40 to 60 feet at the road and the pad at the garage, and keep gravel in between.

Repair or Replace: When the Replacement Cost Is Worth It

Before you spend $10,000 on concrete driveway replacement cost, make sure you need to. A driveway is worth replacing when the problem is in the slab or under it, not on top of it. Four things that point to tear-out instead of another year of patching:

  • Heaving. Panels lifted an inch or more at the joints, usually from clay swelling under a slab that never had a real base. Grinding the edge buys a season. The slab keeps moving.
  • Settling. Sections dropped below the apron or the sidewalk and holding water. Leveling can lift one panel. It can't fix the base that let it drop.
  • Spalling. The top quarter inch flaking off across large areas and exposing aggregate. Common on drives poured thin or finished wet, then hit with 15 winters of deicer. Once it spreads past a few patches, resurfacing peels.
  • Drainage toward the garage. A drive that pitches back toward the house sends water at the foundation every rain. The only fix is to re-establish the fall, and that means a new slab on a regraded base.

Hairline cracks, one settled panel, or surface staining are not reasons to replace. A leveling or sealing company is the right call for those, and we'll tell you so at the site visit. We don't do concrete leveling or patch repair, so we've got no reason to talk you into a tear-out you don't need.

The 5-year test: If you've got three or more panels with structural problems, add up what you'd spend patching, leveling, and sealing over the next 5 years. On most Columbia driveways in that shape, the repair bills pass the cost of a new slab, and at the end of it you still have an old driveway.

Why Concrete Driveway Replacement Cost Runs Higher in Mid-Missouri Than the National Guides Say

National cost sites put a new concrete driveway at $4 to $8 per square foot. Nobody around Columbia is pouring a driveway that'll last at $4 a foot in 2026, and here's why.

Clay. Boone, Callaway, and Cole counties sit on clay that swells when it's wet and shrinks when it's dry. A slab poured straight on it rides that movement until it cracks, which is exactly what happened to the driveway you're replacing. The 4 inches of compacted limestone base that a Columbia driveway needs isn't in a national average built on sandy soil, and it's the single biggest reason concrete driveway replacement cost here runs above those guides.

Freeze-thaw. Mid-Missouri gets 40 to 60 freeze-thaw cycles a winter. That means air-entrained concrete, control joints on a tight spacing, and a base that drains. Skip any of those and the slab spalls in 10 years.

The right-of-way. Inside Columbia city limits, the apron is public property and has to be permitted and poured to city standards. You can check the current requirements on the City of Columbia permits and licensing page. Outside the city, Boone County and MoDOT on state routes have their own approach requirements. We pull the permit and pour to spec so the inspection passes the first time.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to replace a two-car concrete driveway in Columbia, MO?

A typical two-car driveway in Columbia is 600 to 800 square feet, and in 2026 the concrete driveway replacement cost for that size runs $7,000 to $14,000 removed and replaced. That covers saw-cutting the old slab loose, breaking it out and hauling it to a crusher, cutting and compacting the subgrade, placing 4 inches of crushed limestone base, and pouring a 4-inch reinforced slab with a broom finish. The low end is a straight, flat drive with easy truck access and fiber reinforcement. The high end is a longer drive, a deeper base where the old one settled, rebar instead of fiber, or an apron in the city right-of-way that has to be poured to 6 inches.

Is it cheaper to repair or replace a concrete driveway?

Repair is cheaper when the problem is on the surface. Sealing, crack filling, or leveling one settled panel runs a few hundred to a couple thousand dollars and makes sense on a drive that is otherwise sound. Replacement is cheaper over the long run when the problem is in the slab or under it: panels heaving at the joints, multiple sections dropped and holding water, spalling spread across large areas, or a drive that pitches back toward the garage. Patching those buys a season or two, and the money is gone when the slab keeps moving. A good rule is that once you have three or more panels with structural problems, the repair bills over 5 years will pass the cost of a new driveway.

How much does it cost to remove an old concrete driveway?

Removal alone runs $3 to $7 per square foot around Columbia, MO, plus disposal at $40 to $90 per ton as a pass-through. A 700 square foot, 4-inch drive is about 17 tons of concrete, and a 6-inch drive with rebar is closer to 26 tons. So a typical two-car driveway tear-out lands around $2,500 to $5,500 with hauling. What pushes it up is thick, heavily reinforced concrete, tight access where the trucks can't get next to the slab, and a lot of saw-cutting to keep the garage apron and sidewalk clean. On a full replacement, removal is already inside the total, so you only see it as a line item if you ask.

Does a thicker driveway cost a lot more?

Going from 4 inches to 5 or 6 inches adds about $2 per square foot, so $1,200 to $1,600 on a two-car drive. For most residential driveways that only carry cars and pickups, 4 inches of 4,000 psi air-entrained concrete on a compacted rock base is the right spec, and the extra thickness doesn't buy much. If you park an RV, a boat trailer, a loaded work truck, or a dumpster is going to sit on it, 5 to 6 inches with #3 rebar on a 24-inch grid is worth the money. The base matters more than the thickness. A 6-inch slab on soft clay cracks. A 4-inch slab on 4 inches of compacted limestone lasts.

How long does a new concrete driveway last in Missouri?

A concrete driveway poured on a proper base should last 25 to 30 years in Mid-Missouri, and plenty go longer. The ones that fail in 10 to 15 are almost always the ones poured straight on clay with no rock base, finished too wet, or poured thin and then hit with deicer every winter. Freeze-thaw is the enemy here. Air-entrained concrete, control joints cut every 10 to 12 feet, a base that drains, and keeping rock salt off it for the first couple of winters are what get you to 30 years. Sealing every 3 to 5 years helps the surface but doesn't fix a bad base.
